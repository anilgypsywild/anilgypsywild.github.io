Product context

I led research for Compassion Causes, moving the experience from a product-listing browse model toward an outcome-based, mobile-first discovery model, while preserving the trust cues users needed before giving.

Project background

Compassion Causes needed a clearer path from browsing to confident giving.

Product context

Compassion Causes helps supporters discover and fund urgent needs submitted by church partners around the world. The product challenge was not only whether people could donate; it was whether new visitors could quickly understand what causes existed, why they mattered, and which one felt personally meaningful enough to support.

Problem to solve

The live category model was visually consistent but conceptually hard to use. Categories such as Technology, Infrastructure, and Income Generation described how help was delivered or how Compassion organized work internally. Supporters were trying to answer a different question: what human outcome will my donation create?

User problem

Visitors had to translate labels into meaning: a laptop cause might really be about education, a water system about health and basic needs, and a retaining wall about community safety. That extra interpretation burden slowed discovery and weakened emotional momentum.

Product risk

The business risk was early discovery drop-off: users could leave before reaching the moments that build donor confidence: cause detail, trust proof, allocation clarity, and checkout intent.

Research response

I designed a full research arc: baseline usability benchmark, heuristic review, open and closed card sorting, MaxDiff motivation research, and a counterbalanced SBS prototype benchmark against the existing Causes experience.

What users saw Why it created friction Research-backed reframing
Technology category containing causes such as laptops for education, prosthetics for health, and innovation labs. One label bundled unrelated outcomes under an abstract mechanism. Users had to infer what the cause actually changed. Outcome framing: Education access, health/mobility, job skills, or community capacity depending on the cause.
Infrastructure causes such as retaining wall safety, church roof safety, modern bathrooms, or water systems. The label described the asset, not the donor-relevant benefit. Supporters needed to understand safety, sanitation, and basic needs. Outcome framing: Safety and Essential Infrastructure, including clean water, sanitation, health, and community protection.
Income Generation causes such as pig farming livelihood or community garden. Participants often saw income as a downstream outcome, not always a primary cause type. Outcome framing: Skills and Livelihood, with agriculture or income as subcategory language where needed.
Water causes such as water well systems. This category worked better because the label naturally aligned the asset with the human outcome. Preserve the pattern: clear labels work when the mechanism and human outcome are obvious.

Research value

Compassion Causes decisions supported by this research.

1. Advance Story Behind the Story browse

Story Behind the Story (SBS) was statistically clearer for discovery: clarity rose from 5.76 to 6.28 out of 7, p = 0.0133, Cohen dz = 0.36. The strongest result supported the new browse/category direction.

2. Preserve decision cues

Trust, confidence, and SUS did not separate strongly. The recommendation was to carry forward the browse model while retaining urgency, allocation, proof, and cause-detail cues.

3. Refine IA around outcomes

Card sorting showed users grouped causes by outcomes, not internal program language. The IA recommendation was a five-category outcome model with ambiguous cases handled as subcategories.

Representative research engagement

Compassion Causes mixed-methods program for discovery, trust, and conversion.

1. Baseline usability benchmark

I designed a benchmark around the core Compassion Causes consumer journey: discover a cause, understand the cause detail page, simulate donation intent, use filters, evaluate trust indicators, and recover from a search dead end.

  • Methods: unmoderated usability, task-based testing, open-ended probes, controlled failure task.
  • Metrics: task success, time on task, ease, CSAT, NPS, trust confidence.
  • Influence: created a repeatable scorecard and revealed navigation, transparency, and product information gaps.

2. Product information and trust synthesis

The early thematic analysis showed that users needed donation transparency, consistent language, stronger detail pages, and clearer labels before they could confidently continue toward donation.

  • Methods: thematic analysis, journey synthesis, UX tenet mapping.
  • Influence: reframed the problem from “make it easier to browse” to “make the decision feel clear and trustworthy.”

3. Card sorting and taxonomy validation

I translated participant mental models into an outcome-based navigation structure, then validated category hypotheses with a closed sort requested by Product and Design.

  • Methods: open card sort, similarity clustering, entropy review, label normalization, closed card-sort validation.
  • Influence: stabilized a five-category IA and clarified edge cases through subcategory framing.

4. SBS prototype validation and iteration

SBS was a risky redesign because it changed the browse model. I used the prior benchmark task family so the comparison stayed fair and tied to product goals rather than surface-level preference claims.

  • Methods: counterbalanced within-subject Lyssna test, qualitative/video review, paired statistics.
  • Influence: supported SBS browse clarity while preserving existing Causes trust and detail cues.

IA and taxonomy evidence

Card sorting clarified how Compassion Causes supporters expected to browse.

Participants grouped “Water well system” with clean water and basic needs, “Preschool learning center” with education, and “Barber skills training” with livelihood, showing that the browse model needed to lead with outcomes.

Open card sort, n = 23, with similarity clustering, entropy review, and label normalization.

The card-sort work produced a research-backed IA tree with five stable top-level categories: Education and School Development, Skills and Livelihood, Safety and Essential Infrastructure, Community and Youth Spaces, and Arts and Culture.

A follow-up closed card sort tested five Product and Design hypotheses: Education, Training and Skill Building, Income Generation, Safety, and Basic Needs. The output helped separate strong anchor categories from edge cases like health centers, community gardens, pig farming, and arts/culture causes.

IA question Method Decision influenced
Do users group causes by internal program type or human outcome? Open card sort with 23 participants, plus similarity clustering, entropy review, and label normalization. Decision: Move the IA toward outcome-based categories and away from asset/program-name labels.
Can Product and Design's five proposed buckets hold under constraint? Closed card sort with 20 cause cards and predefined categories. Decision: Keep the anchor categories, but resolve ambiguous edge cases with clearer subcategory framing.
How should SBS navigation be validated after taxonomy work? Tree-test-style task paths embedded in prototype testing: find education, urgent/basic-need, country, and outcome-based causes. Decision: Connect IA changes to task success, clarity, and intent-fit metrics in the SBS benchmark.

Example outcome-based navigation model

Instead of asking users to decode internal program names, the IA framed causes around the benefit supporters expected to create.

  • Education and School Development: preschool learning center, literacy program, classroom renovation.
  • Skills and Livelihood: barber skills training, sewing workshop, 3D printing skills lab.
  • Safety and Essential Infrastructure: water well system, modern bathrooms, retaining wall safety.
  • Community and Youth Spaces: safe playground, safe recreation area, community garden.
  • Arts and Culture: music and dance school, heritage workshops.

Example browse paths

These paths turned vague browsing into task-oriented discovery.

  • “I care about school access” → Education and School Development → Literacy Program.
  • “I want basic needs first” → Safety and Essential Infrastructure → Clean Water / Sanitation.
  • “I want long-term self-sufficiency” → Skills and Livelihood → Job Skills Training.
  • “I want a safe place for children” → Community and Youth Spaces → Safe Play Spaces.

Research interpretation: card sorting was used as IA validation, not as an inferential test. With n = 23, agreement, ambiguity, entropy, and clustering identified stable navigation anchors and risky labels. The SBS benchmark then tested whether the resulting browse model improved clarity and intent fit at the experience level.

Card-sort and tree-test bridge

The open sort identified the natural category structure. The closed sort tested whether the proposed buckets worked when users had to choose one. The next validation step used tree-test style top tasks inside the SBS prototype flow. 

# Card-sort analysis logic
# 1. Normalize participant-authored labels.
# 2. Build card-by-card similarity matrix.
# 3. Cluster on distance = 1 - similarity.
# 4. Review entropy to find ambiguous cards.
# 5. Translate stable clusters into navigation labels.

distance <- 1 - similarity_matrix
clusters <- hclust(as.dist(distance), method = "average")
groups <- cutree(clusters, k = 5)

R snippet for comparative usability analysis

# Paired prototype comparison
# Research question: does SBS improve browse clarity
# for the same participants who used the existing Causes browse?

paired <- clean_results |>
  select(participant_id, clarity_sbs, clarity_causes) |>
  mutate(delta_clarity = clarity_sbs - clarity_causes)

t.test(paired$clarity_sbs, paired$clarity_causes, paired = TRUE)
wilcox.test(paired$clarity_sbs, paired$clarity_causes, paired = TRUE)

# Product-friendly effect size for within-subject ratings
cohen_dz <- mean(paired$delta_clarity, na.rm = TRUE) /
  sd(paired$delta_clarity, na.rm = TRUE)

R snippet for MaxDiff motivation modeling

# Best-worst choices become winner-over-loser pairs.
# Difference coding estimates which motivations matter most.

fit_overall <- glm(
  y ~ . - 1,
  family = binomial(),
  data = cbind(y, X)
)

utilities <- coef(fit_overall)
relative_importance <- scales::rescale(
  utilities - mean(utilities, na.rm = TRUE),
  to = c(0, 100)

Results

Story Behind the Story benchmark results for Compassion Causes browse redesign.

Story Behind the Story (SBS) was the outcome-based browse prototype: a redesigned way to help supporters find causes by impact area, need, and relevance. The benchmark compared SBS against the existing Compassion Causes browse experience.

Strategic surprise

Prospects needed clarity and control before emotional storytelling.

The MaxDiff results challenged the obvious messaging instinct. For first-time prospects, the strongest product move was not leading with urgency, faith, or emotional imagery. It was showing stewardship first: where the money goes, what impact it creates, how much control the donor has, and how simple the giving path feels.

Product implication: Compassion Causes needed a product information hierarchy that led with clarity, tangible impact, autonomy, and ease before layering deeper faith or story-based appeals. This directly informed the SBS direction: clearer browse categories, outcome language, and a mobile-first discovery model that reduced the work required to find a meaningful cause.

Product leadership influence

Executive and product leadership used this research to guide Compassion Causes strategy.

VP / executive alignment

Framed evidence for senior product and design stakeholders in terms of product risk, strategic upside, confidence, and what could responsibly move forward.

Decision architecture

Used a 60 / 40 decision threshold, confidence intervals, effect sizes, and qualitative evidence to separate directional signal from launch-risk claims.

Product direction

Influenced the next product direction: use Story Behind the Story for clearer first-view discovery, preserve existing Causes urgency/proof/detail cues for donor confidence, and use production telemetry to evaluate new-user conversion.

The research showed that Story Behind the Story improved browse clarity (6.28 vs 5.76 / 7, p = 0.0133) and intent-fit preference (31 vs 14, p = 0.0161), while trust and confidence remained less differentiated. That evidence supported a hybrid product direction: use SBS for discovery, preserve existing Causes urgency/proof/detail cues, and validate new-user conversion in production.
