1. Purpose

To reimagine the first-time donor Out-of-Box Experience (OOBE) for Compassion Causes, drawing inspiration from Home Depot’s ability to transform a first use into a trust-building and habit-forming experience.

This work integrates three layers of insight:

  1. HCI theory on first-use experiences and trust formation [1–3],
  2. Behavioral UX evidence from Home Depot’s OOBE (predictable, guided, reassuring), and
  3. Empirical Compassion Causes data from the 2025 MaxDiff Sponsor Motivation Study [4].

The goal is not to prescribe interface design, but to define how the first 90 seconds of interaction should feel, reassure, and inspire to turn a curious visitor into a confident donor.

“Reimagining the Compassion Causes OOBE: From Clarity to Conviction — How Trust, Transparency, and Joy Define the First 90 Seconds of Giving.”

💝 2. Theoretical Foundation: OOBE in Human-Computer Interaction

  1. Forlizzi & Battarbee (2004) [1] define experience as unfolding through anticipation, encounter, use, and reflection. The first encounter establishes emotional and cognitive trust.
  2. McCarthy & Wright (2004) [2] expand this: OOBE is the beginning of a story users tell about meaning and value.
  3. Hassenzahl (2010) [3] unites pragmatic clarity and hedonic joy: the first interaction must both “work” and feel right.

In short:

An OOBE is not a feature—it’s a first impression of purpose.
For Compassion Causes, this means the donor’s first minute must communicate clarity, security, and meaning, before any emotional appeal.

3. Prior Research Findings

The 2025 MaxDiff Sponsor Motivation Study [4] found:

  • Clarity of purpose and transparency ranked as the strongest motivators (78% share).
  • Control and trust outranked emotion or faith-based cues for new donors.
  • Repeat givers reported that visible impact tracking and ease of navigation increased long-term loyalty.

This data echoes the HCI OOBE principles: before inspiration, donors seek orientation. Before emotion, they need evidence of integrity.

🍬 5. Key Learnings from HomeDepot's OOBE

A. Clarity as the Foundation of Trust (Forlizzi & Battarbee [1])

The OOBE must minimize friction and uncertainty.
Causes Application:

  • Preload content by donor’s region or preferred cause.
  • Use plain-language CTAs (“Give,” “Track Impact”).
  • Reduce decisions early to build momentum.
    (4D Focus: Doability + Dependability)

B. The Emotional Arc of First Use (McCarthy & Wright [2])

Every first experience is a narrative.
Causes Application:

  • The first donation flow should feel like a chapter in an impact story.
  • Use short, emotionally grounding copy (“Your kindness begins here”).
    (4D Focus: Delight + Desire)

C. Joy as Reinforcement (Hassenzahl [3])

Satisfaction grows when action feels personally meaningful.
Causes Application:

  • Progress bar → “Lives Touched” or “Impact Delivered.”
  • Gratitude message → subtle, honest, personal.
    (4D Focus: Delight + Desire)

D. Existing Research with MaxDiff Validates Donor, Prospect Motivations for Causes

Quantitatively, donors value clarity before emotion.
Causes Application:

  • Emphasize transparency first; inspiration second.
  • Make trust visible through progress indicators and real-time updates.

🔊 7. Tldr;

  1. Home Depot’s OOBE: Builds trust through consistency, structure, and micro-feedback loops.
  2. Compassion Causes’ OOBE: Should build conviction through clarity and inspiration through authenticity.

The two journeys mirror each other in form but diverge in meaning:

  • Home Depot: “You can trust us with your purchase.”
  • Compassion Causes: “You can trust us with your purpose.”

📚 Appendix A: Theoretical Foundations of OOBE in Human-Computer Interaction.

[1] Jodi Forlizzi and Katja Battarbee. 2004. Understanding Experience in Interactive Systems. DIS ’04.

_ACM. DOI: _0.1145/1013115.1013152

Key Points from the Article

  • Forlizzi and Battarbee describe experience as unfolding across four temporal stages: anticipation, first encounter, use, and reflection.
  • They emphasize that the first encounter (OOBE) determines whether users interpret the system as trustworthy, usable, and emotionally coherent.
  • Experience is both designed and emergent: users bring expectations, context, and emotion that color their first use.
  • Designers should orchestrate early interactions to produce predictability, low anxiety, and positive affect, creating a durable emotional baseline.

What This Means for research team

For Compassion Causes, the first 90 seconds of a donor’s journey are equivalent to a product’s OOBE.

  • The app must set expectations, build trust, and evoke purpose before any financial commitment is made.
  • This early touchpoint should convey: “You are in safe hands; your impact matters.”
  • In the research team’s 4D terms:
    • Doability: reduce early friction with intuitive discovery and simplified onboarding.
    • Dependability: signal authenticity (secure payments, verified stories, clear impact flows).
  • When these are present, the first encounter becomes emotionally sticky—donors interpret the brand as credible and compassionate.

[2] John McCarthy and Peter Wright. 2004. Technology as Experience. MIT Press.

Key Points from the Book

  • McCarthy and Wright frame experience as a narrative process—each interaction contributes to a user’s ongoing story about meaning and self.
  • The OOBE is the prologue to that story: the user decides whether the technology aligns with their personal values.
  • They introduce four threads of experience: compositional (structure), emotional, sensual, and spatio-temporal.
  • Good design harmonizes these threads, producing not just ease of use but felt significance.

What This Means for research team

In the context of Compassion Causes:

  • The OOBE should invite donors into a narrative of shared purpose, not just present tasks or forms.
  • Early visuals, tone, and copy (“You are changing a life today”) engage the emotional and compositional threads of experience.
  • This approach transforms the donation flow from transactional to transformative.
  • Within the 4D framework:
    • Delight: emerge from emotional resonance (hope, compassion, belonging).
    • Desire: sustained through narrative continuity—seeing one’s role in an ongoing story of impact.

[3] Marc Hassenzahl. 2010. Experience Design: Technology for All the Right Reasons. Morgan & Claypool.

_DOI: _10.2200/S00261ED1V01Y201003HCI008

Key Points from the Book

  • Hassenzahl distinguishes pragmatic qualities (usefulness, efficiency) from hedonic qualities (stimulation, identification, pleasure).
  • The first use moment—OOBE—strongly shapes users’ hedonic perception of trust and joy.
  • Users judge a product not only by function but by how it makes them feel about themselves while using it.
  • Successful experience design ensures goal achievement feels personally rewarding—not just completed.

What This Means for research team

For Compassion Causes:

  • OOBE must balance functional ease (“I can donate quickly and securely”) with emotional fulfillment (“I feel joy and pride in helping”).
  • Micro-interactions—like progress bars or gratitude screens—should connect doing good with feeling good.
  • In 4D UX terms:
    • Doability ensures donors reach success easily.
    • Delight turns that success into meaning.
    • Desire grows from feeling emotionally rewarded for participation.
  • Every tap and confirmation reinforces identity alignment: “I am the kind of person who helps others.”

🖌️ Appendix B: Home Depot OOBE Screens

Annotated UX Insights for Compassion Causes

Screen 1: App Launch — Brand Immersion

Description: Displays Home Depot logo and tagline "How doers get more done" with animated icons.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Establishes instant brand recognition and purpose.
  • Delight: Clean minimalist motion gives confidence and emotional clarity.
  • Implication: Compassion Causes can create a similar branded launch moment: short visual cue signaling mission (e.g., “How giving gets more done”).

Screen 2: Predictive Search & Intent Recognition

Description: Predictive search suggestions populate instantly when typing (e.g., Home Depot, Hobby Lobby, Hotspot Shield).
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Reduces friction by anticipating intent.
  • Desire: Feels responsive and personal.
  • Implication: Causes could use similar predictive recommendations to surface local causes or giving categories as users type (e.g., “children”, “clean water”, “education”).

Screen 3: Store Setup — Location Permission Prompt

Description: Prompts user to allow Home Depot to access their location for personalized inventory and offers.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Transparent, contextual permissioning builds trust.
  • Delight: Clear purpose—"so we can show nearby stores"—reduces privacy anxiety.
  • Implication: Introduce geo-context to causes (e.g., “show causes near you” or “projects in your time zone”).

Screen 4: Store Selection Map

Description: Map and list of nearby stores with distances, addresses, and CTA: Set West Reno as My Store.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Streamlined setup via geolocation.
  • Delight: Combines autonomy and assistance.
  • Implication: Causes can localize giving (“Set your giving region”) for personalized community impact stories.

Screen 5: Notification Prompt — Order Updates

Description: Encourages user to enable notifications for updates like pickup readiness and local offers.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Clear, benefit-driven consent (not spammy).
  • Desire: Offers emotional reassurance via control.
  • Implication: Frame notifications around impact updates (e.g., “Receive updates when your sponsored child’s project progresses”).

Screen 6: Personalization Prompt — Discover Products You’ll Love

Description: Asks for permission to enable personalized ads.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Uses honesty and voluntary choice.
  • Delight: Keeps design clean; minimal overload.
  • Implication: Replace ad personalization with mission personalization (e.g., “Help us show you causes that align with your heart”).

Screen 7: Account Creation — Do More with a Home Depot Account

Description: Simple tri-option: Sign In, Create Account, or Not Now.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Low cognitive load; progressive engagement.
  • Desire: Emotional copy (“Do more”) drives motivation.
  • Implication: Causes could adopt “Do More with Your Giving” CTA at similar journey points.

Screen 8: Verification — Email Code Entry

Description: Email-based code entry for verification.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Straightforward trust signal through transparency.
  • Delight: Simple linear flow keeps users confident.
  • Implication: Replicate 2-step verification for donor security without breaking flow.

Screen 9: Biometric Login Prompt

Description: Prompts Face ID / Fingerprint setup post-verification.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Optional trust feature—reduces friction for returning users.
  • Delight: Feels premium and safe.
  • Implication: Offer Face ID for quick re-login to giving account.

Screen 10: Product Page & Add to Cart

Description: Simple product view showing availability, store location, and Add to Cart CTA.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Clarity in purchase path.
  • Desire: Transparent local stock builds confidence.
  • Implication: For Causes, show availability of funding opportunities (e.g., “$50 left to complete this project”).

Screen 11: Purchase History + Past Orders Summary

Description: Displays completed order details with thumbnail previews and total cost.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Reinforces completion and proof of action.
  • Delight: Encourages user reflection and satisfaction.
  • Implication: Replace with “Impact History”—show completed donations and outcomes.

Screen 12: Order Details & Fulfillment Transparency

Description: Visual order tracking bar with stages: Order Received → Ready for Pickup → Picked Up.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Progress visualization enhances user control.
  • Delight: Clear visual metaphor for completion.
  • Implication: Map donation lifecycle using similar step progress bars (Given → In Progress → Impact Delivered).

Screen 13: OOBE: Intercept Survey

Description: In-app micro-survey with 5-point ease-of-use scale.
UX Takeaways:

  • Doability: Effortless participation; reduces survey fatigue.
  • Desire: Instant gratification via quick completion.
  • Implication: Add micro-intercepts after donation confirmation (“How easy was it to support this cause today?”) for UX Scorecard calibration.

Screen 14: OOBE: Pickup Experience

Description: Series of Likert items evaluating pickup process timing, product condition, and ease.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Detailed quantitative insights on operational UX.
  • Doability: Streamlined structure avoids drop-off.
  • Implication: Causes can mirror this for post-donation transparency surveys (“Was your giving experience clear and trustworthy?”).

Screen 15: OOBE: Employee Experience Feedback

Description: Human-centric survey recognizing employees who went beyond expectations.
UX Takeaways:

  • Delight: Emotional reciprocity strengthens loyalty.
  • Desire: Encourages gratitude-based engagement.
  • Implication: Causes can include praise prompts for Frontline Church Partners (“Did someone’s story or effort inspire you today?”).

Screen 16: OOBE: Final Impression

Description: Free-text field for final feedback.
UX Takeaways:

  • Dependability: Open expression increases emotional closure.
  • Delight: Completes user journey with agency.
  • Implication: Offer space for reflection post-donation (“Share why this cause moved you”).

💰 Executive Summary

Home Depot’s OOBE exemplifies progressive trust-building through small, emotionally intelligent UX cues—permissioning, personalization, verification, and reflection. Applying the 4D Framework, Causes can:

  1. Doability: Simplify giving setup and region personalization.
  2. Dependability: Reinforce transparency through progress visuals.
  3. Delight: Celebrate completion moments via micro-intercepts.
  4. Desire: Deepen relationship through emotional feedback loops.

This structure offers a measurable blueprint for Causes’ OOBE redesign grounded in behavioral confidence and trust calibration.
