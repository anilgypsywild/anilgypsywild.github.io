Research Insights by Email-Template for Designers, UX-Neighbours

1. Featured Cause

5s Test

  • Majority of respondents identified the email as soliciting donations for charitable causes, with specific mentions of poverty, children, and Compassion International.
    • ~40% of responses explicitly mentioned "donating" or "donations" as the core purpose
    • ~20% specifically referenced helping children in poverty.
  • The connection to agricultural or food production support was noted by several respondents who mentioned crop production, gardening, or hydroponics.
    • A number of respondents (~20%) indicated they didn't have enough time to fully process the email content, suggesting the viewing duration may have been insufficient for complete comprehension.
  • Compassion International was specifically identified as the sender organization by multiple respondents, with indications that the email was targeting previous donors by suggesting additional causes they might support beyond their initial contribution.

FirstClick

Key Objective of the Email

Encourage supporters to click into a cause detail page (specifically “Hydroponic Crop Production”) and potentially donate again - a re-engagement flow.

First Click Insights via HeatMap

1. High-Intensity Clicks on “Take A Look” CTA
The primary concentration is centered on the “Take A Look” button below the Hydroponic Crop description.
Interpretation: This is a strong signal of correct CTA recognition, users understood the intent and clicked on the expected action.

2. Secondary Clicks on “Read More” in Cause Description
A lighter click cluster appears over the “read more” link in the Hydroponic Crop text block.
Interpretation: Some users are seeking more information before taking action. Indicates interest but possible hesitation due to lack of content depth or immediate clarity.

3. Minimal Clicks on Header or Paragraph Intro
No major engagement with the intro paragraph or the “Here’s a similar cause…” heading.
Interpretation: Users are visually skipping straight to the image and cause card, confirming effective visual hierarchy.

4. Tertiary Clicks on “Explore More Causes” CTA (Bottom)
A small heat signature is visible on the footer CTA.
Interpretation: Some users are looking for alternative actions or broader exploration rather than the single recommended cause. Suggests this CTA might be better positioned as a secondary action or fallback for less convinced users.

Overall Interpretation

  • The primary CTA (“Take A Look”) is effective and captures most user attention.
  • Visual hierarchy is functioning well; users are engaging directly with the cause content.

Survey

1.What action is this email encouraging you to take?

  • Learning about mission and donating are key actions encouraged
  • Among 50 respondents, the email primarily encourages users to learn about Compassion's mission (62%) and donate to a related cause (54%). Secondary actions include sharing the cause with others (20%) and reading a project update (18%). Only 4% of respondents (2 people) selected "Other" actions.
  • The data suggests the email effectively communicates dual purposes of education and financial support, while placing less emphasis on sharing or updates.

2.How likely are you to click and explore this featured cause? (1–7)

  • High engagement potential with 76% likely to explore
  • The survey data on likelihood to explore a featured cause shows strong potential for user engagement.
  • A substantial majority (76%) of respondents indicated high likelihood to explore (ratings 5-7), with 30% giving the highest possible rating of 7 and another 26% rating it a 6.
  • Only 14% expressed low likelihood (ratings 1-3), while 10% remained neutral with a rating of 4.
  • All 50 survey participants provided responses, indicating a complete dataset with no missing values.

3.Imagine you had $25 to give this month. Based on this email, how much would you consider donating to this cause or project? (Marginal Willing To Pay)

Quantitative Interpretation

Top-line stats:

  • 34% of users chose the maximum ($25)
  • 66% would give something, but only 2% would give more than $25
  • 28% would give $10 or less, showing moderate conviction
  • 16% would give nothing or opt out entirely ($0 or "I would not give again")

Implications:

  • The email effectively drives donation intent, with most users willing to give a non-trivial amount.
  • However, the lack of users giving more than $25 and 12% selecting $0 indicate that the email could do more to inspire confidence and urgency for larger gifts.
  • The “10–15” cluster suggests modest emotional pull or budget-conscious consideration- not disinterest, but maybe uncertainty or lack of strong motivation.

Qualitative Interpretation

Themes from open-text justifications:

  1. Budget Calibration:
    Many respondents balance generosity with personal financial limitations, dividing giving across organizations. This suggests the email must clearly position this cause as uniquely urgent or impactful to stand out.
  2. Trust-Building Behavior:
    Some respondents start with small gifts and increase over time based on trust and impact. This highlights the importance of reinforcing credibility and transparency in future touchpoints (e.g., updates, thank-you emails).
  3. Strategic Giving:
    Several users apply intentional strategies (e.g., percent of income, test-first, mission alignment), which supports a persona segmentation strategy. Users may need tailored reinforcement depending on whether they're first-time or repeat givers.
  4. Perceived Impact Drives Upper-Range Giving:
    Users who gave $25 or more tended to feel a strong connection to the cause or recognized its tangible outcomes. This confirms that clear, specific impact language (what $25 actually funds) can lift donation intent.

Research Recommendations

  • Enhance emotional urgency and clarity of impact in the copy, especially for the primary ask (what does $25 do?).
    • Include specific, visual outcomes.
  • Reinforce trust cues in future emails: link to project history, completion rate, testimonials.
  • Test segmented asks: “First time supporting?” or “Already made a difference here?” to tailor messaging by relationship stage.

4. Why did they choose that amount?

  • Most people would donate 10−25 of their available funds
  • Survey data reveals 34% of respondents would donate the full $25 available to them, showing strong commitment to the cause.
  • When combined with those willing to give $10, $15, and $20, a total of 70% would contribute at least $10.
  • Only 12% would give nothing, while another 12% would give $5. Just 4% indicated they would not give again, suggesting overall positive sentiment toward continued support of the cause.

2. About Compassion

5s Test

  • Respondents predominantly identify this email as being about a charitable organization called Compassion International, with most recognizing it focuses on helping children, particularly those in poverty.
  • The primary themes identified include: soliciting donations (~22% explicitly mention donations/fundraising), supporting children in need (~40% reference helping/supporting children), and communicating the organization's mission (~20% specifically mention learning about the mission or purpose of the organization).
  • Many responses (~16%) specifically associate the charity's work with international aid, particularly in Africa, while others note it appears to be a faith-based organization. Several respondents indicated they had limited time to review the email content, suggesting the key messaging was quickly identifiable despite brief exposure.

FirstClick

Key Objective of the Email

To educate supporters about Compassion’s mission, build trust, and guide users to explore sponsorship and giving options. It’s less of a donation push and more about creating long-term understanding and connection.

First Click Insights

1. Primary Cluster on “Learn About Compassion International” CTA (Top Button)
The strongest heat zone is centered on the blue CTA button beneath the hero section.
Interpretation: This is a strong indicator that the headline + image + paragraph combo successfully drove users to click the intended button. The button placement and clarity seem effective.

2. Light Interest on “Learn More” Links in the Resource Section
There’s noticeable but limited activity on the “Learn More,” “Join Us,” and “Show Me How” links in the Resources block.
Interpretation: Some users are engaging with the secondary links, indicating curiosity about additional ways to engage. However, the overall click volume here is low compared to the primary CTA, suggesting these could benefit from improved visual hierarchy or differentiation.

3. Lower Click Activity on Impact Stats
The middle section showing “29 countries,” “8,500+ church partners,” and “2.2 million+ supported” shows minimal click interaction.
Interpretation: Users likely recognize this as informational rather than actionable content. These numbers may help build credibility but do not prompt clicks on their own.

4. Footer Buttons ("Read More Stories" and "View Causes") See Some Activity
These links received light attention.
Interpretation: These may serve as fallback or secondary exploration points, useful for users who scroll through the entire email. Still, they are not competing with the primary CTA, which is positive.

Overall Interpretation

  • The hero CTA performs well and is doing the bulk of engagement work.
  • The email functions primarily as an onboarding or primer, not a high-intent transactional message.
  • Supporting links in the lower content blocks (Resources section) could be made more prominent or visual to compete with the hero.

Survey

1.What is the main goal of this email?

  • Email primarily seeks donations with secondary sponsorship focus
  • The data from 50 respondents shows that donation is the dominant perceived goal of the email, with 70% (35 respondents) selecting this option.
  • Sponsorship is the second most identified goal, selected by 40% (20 respondents).
  • Updates were identified by only 18% (9 respondents) as a goal, while 14% (7 respondents) indicated "Other" purposes.
  • Since respondents could select multiple options, the email appears to have multiple goals with donation solicitation being the most prominent objective.

2.How curious or motivated are you to learn more about Compassion? (1–7)

  • 74% of respondents (37 out of 50) indicated strong curiosity about compassion with ratings of 5-7 on the scale.
  • Only 14% (7 respondents) showed low interest with ratings of 1-3, while 12% (6 respondents) were neutral with a rating of 4.
  • The highest level of motivation (rating 7) was selected by more than a third of all participants (36%), making it the single most common response.
  • This demonstrates a substantial interest in learning more about Compassion among the surveyed population.

3. This email guided me clearly to the next step. (1–7)

  • Strong agreement that email provides clear next steps
  • A significant majority of respondents (84%) rated the email positively (5-7) on guiding them clearly to the next step, with nearly half (48%) giving the highest possible rating of 7.
  • Only 6% responded negatively (1-3), showing strong consensus about the email's clarity.
  • The remaining 10% were neutral (rating 4), suggesting the email is effectively guiding most users through their journey with clear next steps.

4. Was there anything unclear or too detailed in this email?

  • Most found email clear with some usability and transparency concerns
  • The majority of respondents (~70%) found the email clear, straightforward, and easy to understand with no issues reported. However, several users noted specific concerns about usability, with one mentioning they "had no idea that I could scroll down to see more" and another finding the email "too brief" with insufficient information on the landing page.
  • Some respondents (~10%) expressed concerns about transparency regarding how donations are allocated, with specific questions about administrative costs and "exactly how/where your money is going." A few users (~8%) mentioned the email contained too many options or choices, making it unclear what action to take, with one noting "too many choices for next steps" and another feeling disconnected between the various sections.
  • Three respondents suggested simplifying the content, with one recommending it "could be summarized more concisely" for an email format, while another suggested making donation instructions more straightforward.

  • Most respondents identify this as an informational newsletter email from a charity organization, specifically Compassion International, providing updates on their charitable activities and impact.
  • The responses consistently mention two key purposes: updating supporters on ongoing efforts (~50% mention updates/newsletters) and soliciting donations for various causes (~30% explicitly mention donations or support requests). Several respondents specifically note the email shows how previous donations are making an impact.
  • A significant number of responses indicate the email contains stories about children being helped and information about specific charitable initiatives. Some respondents (~10%) specifically mention the email offering subscription options for regular updates, suggesting this may be an initial or follow-up communication establishing an ongoing relationship.

FirstClick

Key Objective of the Email

Encourage supporters to browse and engage with multiple causes, surfacing urgency and personal relevance across categories like education, faith, and disaster response.

First Click Insights

1. Concentrated Clicks on First “View” CTA Under Urgent Need (Music Academy)
The most intense click activity centers on the blue “View” button under the Music Academy section labeled “Urgent Need.”
Interpretation: This suggests that the visual structure and urgency labeling effectively draw user attention. Users tend to engage with the top-listed cause, likely due to layout prominence and emotional framing.

2. Secondary Clicks on “View All Urgent Causes”
The link directly under the Music Academy card receives the second-most click activity.
Interpretation: Users are interested in exploring more causes, not just the highlighted one. This validates the inclusion of a broader browsing CTA. It may also indicate that users are unsure which individual cause to pick and would prefer to explore options first.

3. Limited Engagement With Lower “Almost Funded” and “Passionate About” Sections
The lower sections, especially “View” buttons under thematic categories like education and art, see some scattered clicks but not nearly as concentrated.
Interpretation: Scroll fatigue may be at play, or users may perceive the initial urgent cause as more compelling. Alternatively, layout hierarchy may be unintentionally minimizing the importance of these categories.

4. Minor Clicks on “Fully Funded” Section at the Bottom
Almost no engagement is observed on the celebration or thank-you message.
Interpretation: This confirms that once users have scrolled to the bottom, their intention has dropped — this space is better used for gratitude or passive reinforcement, not CTAs.

Overall Interpretation

  • The urgent cause at the top works: it commands attention and engagement.
  • Supporters are curious to explore, as evidenced by clicks on “View All” links. Consider elevating those links or testing card grouping by theme with better scannability.
  • Lower modules see less interaction. Consider progressive disclosure (accordion style or collapsed states) or rotating cause categories to avoid scroll fatigue.
  • Celebration content (Fully Funded) is being ignored, suggesting it may be more effective as an interstitial moment in other contexts (e.g., a confirmation email) rather than a scroll-bottom element.

Survey

1.What’s the primary purpose of this email?

  • Donation solicitation is primary email purpose
  • The primary purpose of the email appears to be donation solicitation, with 68% (34 out of 50) of respondents selecting "Donate" as an option.
  • Reading content was the second most common purpose at 52% (26 respondents), followed by browsing at 36% (18 respondents).
  • Since this was a multiple-choice checkbox question where respondents could select multiple purposes, the total exceeds 100%.
  • Only 8% (4 respondents) indicated "Other" purposes beyond the main categories provided.

2.How likely are you to explore these causes?(1–7)

  • High willingness to explore causes with 78% positive response
  • The data shows a strong positive inclination toward exploring causes, with 78% of respondents (39 out of 50) selecting ratings of 5 or higher on the 7-point scale.
  • The highest response rate was for the maximum rating of 7 (Most Likely), chosen by 34% (17 respondents), followed by equal proportions (22% each) selecting ratings of 5 and 6. Only 12% of respondents (6 people) indicated a low likelihood (ratings of 1 or 3) of exploring these causes, with no respondents selecting a rating of 2.
  • The middle rating of 4 was selected by 10% (5 respondents), suggesting some moderate interest.

3.This email was well organized and easy to scan. (1–7)

  • Strong positive perception of email organization and scannability
  • An overwhelming 90% of respondents rated the email organization and scannability positively (5-7 on the scale), with nearly half (46%) giving the highest possible rating of 7.
  • Only 4% of respondents gave a negative rating of 2, while 6% remained neutral with a score of 4. The strong positive skew (76% rating it 6 or 7) suggests the email format successfully prioritized clarity and scannable structure for the vast majority of recipients.

4. What made you want (or not want) to click anything in this email?

  • Visual appeal and organization drive clicks, while too many choices overwhelm
  • Compelling visuals and organization were primary motivators for engagement, with users specifically mentioning photographs (~14% of respondents) and clear visual elements like blue buttons that "stand out" and create an intuitive interface.
  • The "Urgent Need" messaging created a sense of importance that motivated several users to click, with multiple respondents specifically citing urgency as their reason for wanting to learn more.
  • Content organization and cause specificity played a significant role, with users appreciating the clear breakdown of categories (like "urgent causes" and music education), making it easier to find relevant opportunities.
  • Conversely, ~16% of respondents felt overwhelmed by too many choices and projects, stating they "felt overwhelmed by viewing all causes" and would prefer fewer, more categorized options. Some mentioned that excessive scrolling reduced their likelihood of seeing all content.
  • Personal relevance was important for engagement, with several users mentioning they clicked because the causes aligned with their interests (particularly music education) or because they wanted to understand exactly where their donations would go.

Progress update on a previously supported donation project

Many specifically mentioned the hydroponic farming/crop production focus of the project. Several respondents noted that beyond providing an update, the email encourages further engagement through either additional donations or sharing the cause with others.
  • The communication appears to be from Compassion International, with responses indicating the email effectively communicates both progress achieved through prior donations and creates opportunities for continued support. The dual purpose of reporting impact ("how my donation helped") and soliciting further action was clearly recognized by multiple respondents, suggesting the email successfully balances information sharing with calls to action.

FirstClick

Key Objective of the Email

Reinforce the donor’s impact by showing progress on a cause they supported (via a fundraiser), and encourage continued engagement or sharing with others.

First Click Insights

1. Strong Engagement on “View Update” CTA
The most intense click activity is centered on the primary CTA button below the photo gallery (“View Update”).
Interpretation: Users immediately recognized the call to action and were drawn to the expected next step. The content above it (project images + heading) likely provides sufficient context to drive that behavior. This suggests effective CTA placement and clarity.

2. Minimal Clicks on “Share Your Cause” CTA
The “Share Your Cause” button in the “What’s Next?” section receives noticeably less attention.
Interpretation: While well-placed visually, this secondary CTA may feel less relevant in a progress update context. Many users may interpret this as a low-priority or promotional action, rather than something immediately meaningful to them.

3. Scattered Clicks on Satisfaction Survey (CSAT)
Clicks are dispersed along the 1–7 scale at the bottom (with a cluster near 7).
Interpretation: This shows that users are at least interacting with the follow-up sentiment metric, though possibly influenced by layout positioning. The relatively even spread suggests either indecision or lack of emotional clarity about the message.

4. No Significant Clicks on Intro Copy or Photo Gallery Itself
The header text ("We have an exciting update…") and image thumbnails see negligible engagement.
Interpretation: Users may interpret this content as passive (non-clickable), or they may be skimming quickly past text to find a CTA. This implies a potential opportunity to test clickable image behaviors or to highlight the story within the image captions more directly.

Overall Interpretation

  • The primary CTA is working well: “View Update” earns dominant attention and serves the core function of this message.
  • Sharing is deprioritized: Users aren’t organically motivated to share — that may require stronger contextual framing (“Make an even bigger impact”) or placement closer to the update content.
  • Satisfaction score interaction is good but scattered: Consider clearer framing or placing it after content that reinforces value/impact.
  • Intro copy and thumbnails are visually engaging but not actionable: May be useful to test interactive elements (e.g., click-to-enlarge, inline captions) or bold subheadlines to elevate key takeaways.

4. What part of the update helped you connect? or What was missing?

Summative UX Metrics

SUS: 82.7

While we did not administer the exact 10-item SUS, we extracted five validated items from the full SUPR-Q battery that map directly to SUS dimensions: usability, clarity, trust, confidence, and relevance.

Questions Used

  1. This email was easy to understand.
  2. I understood what I was supposed to do after reading this email.
  3. I trust the information provided in this email
  4. The content in this email was relevant and useful to me.
  5. The design of the email is visually appealing.

Average SUS-equivalent score: 82.7

This places the email experience in the top quartile of usability benchmarks, corresponding to:Grade: A, Adjective Rating: Excellent, Acceptability: Clearly acceptable

CSAT: 5.82

Interpretation

  • The overall satisfaction score (mean: 5.82 on a 7-point scale) reflects strong positive sentiment toward Compassion Causes emails as a communication tool.
  • Nearly 70% of users rated their satisfaction as 6 or 7, suggesting the emails are both effective and appreciated by the majority of users.
  • Only 6% of respondents selected a rating below the midpoint (1–3), indicating minimal dissatisfaction.
  • Meanwhile, 12% rated their experience as neutral (4), suggesting a smaller segment is neither displeased nor strongly engaged.
  • The distribution shows a strong mode at 7 ("Very Satisfied"), with 38% of responses, followed closely by 32% at 6.
  • This clustering in the upper range confirms that most users view these emails as a valuable way to stay informed and connected with the platform.

Implications

  1. The email experience is performing well and shows no critical usability or satisfaction issues. It is ready for scale-up or additional testing in live environments.
  2. Users who rated the emails as neutral (4) represent a segment that could be further engaged through improved personalization, clearer CTA structure, or stronger emotional resonance in the message.
  3. Given the low dissatisfaction rate, future design iterations can focus less on fixing broken experiences and more on optimizing content hierarchy and deepening user connection to the cause.
  4. This CSAT result can serve as a benchmark for future email iterations or A/B tests. An increase from 5.82 can be interpreted as a measurable gain in emotional or functional engagement.

NPS: 7.24

Quantitative Analysis

  • Total respondents: 50
  • Mean score: 7.24
  • Promoters (score 9–10): 31 respondents (62%)
  • Passives (score 7–8): 10 respondents (20%)
  • Detractors (score 0–6): 9 respondents (18%)
  • Net Promoter Score (NPS): NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors = 62% − 18% = +44

This is a strong NPS, well above typical nonprofit benchmarks, which often range from +20 to +40.

Interpretation

  • The NPS distribution shows a highly positive experience with Compassion Causes emails.
  • A significant 62% of respondents fall into the Promoter category (rating 9 or 10), indicating they are enthusiastic and likely to recommend receiving these emails to others.
    • The largest cluster (30%) gave the maximum score of 10, reflecting strong emotional or functional satisfaction.
    • Another 18% gave a score of 9, reinforcing that the upper bound of sentiment is well populated.
  • The percentage of Detractors is low (18%), and spread across low scores (0–6), without clustering heavily around the extreme low end. This suggests there are relatively few deeply dissatisfied users, and most users find the experience either valuable or at least unobjectionable.
  • The 20% of users classified as Passives (scores 7–8) are satisfied but not enthusiastic. This group represents an opportunity to convert neutral sentiment into advocacy.

Implications

  1. A Net Promoter Score of +44 reflects a healthy and well-received email experience.
  2. The high percentage of promoters provides a strong foundation for community advocacy, fundraising momentum, and peer-sharing features, especially when tied to emotionally resonant updates or calls to action.
  3. The small number of detractors should still be examined for patterns in user confusion, content overload, or a mismatch between expectations and email tone or design.
  4. The 7–8 passive segment indicates an area for potential optimization. These users could be influenced positively by more dynamic content, clearer CTAs, or more targeted cause relevance.
  5. This NPS result can serve as a baseline benchmark. If future iterations of email design, storytelling, or personalization are tested, they should aim to exceed +44 or lift the mean from 7.24 toward 8+.
