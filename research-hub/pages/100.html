\sum Summative Metrics

Usability in the Human-Computer Interaction World (HCI)

[SOURCE:ISO 9241-11: 1998]

Extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use Usefulness, Ease of use, Learnability, Like-ability are user dimensions to be measured.

[B] Behavioral Metrics

Behavioral metrics measure the noted behavior logged in a system. Behavioral metrics measure

  • Wholistic usability of the system
  • Efficiency of the Information Architecture
  • Task performance of users with our system

Here, the system can be an application, a service, or a feature under the research lens.

B1. Measuring System Usability

We measure the usability of the entire system with this standardized, validated, scale used extensively in HCI research since the 1980's called System Usability Scale or SUS

SUS has the following ten questions, and the response to the questions can be converted into a score from 0 to 100 to interpret the overall usability with optoins show below from Grades through Adjective desciptors.

  1. I think that I would like to use this system frequently.
  2. I found the system unnecessarily complex.
  3. I thought the system was easy to use.
  4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.
  5. I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.
  6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.
  7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.
  8. I found the system very cumbersome to use.
  9. I felt very confident using the system.
  10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.

User Metric for User Experience (UMUX)

Dimension Info
Purpose Assess the perceived usability of a product
Items Four statements on a 7-point Likert scale
Statements 1. [This system’s] capabilities meet my requirements. 2. Using [this system] is a frustrating experience. 3. [This system] is easy to use. 4. I have to spend too much time correcting things with [this system].
Goal Provide results similar to the System Usability Scale (SUS)
Benefits Can be integrated into product development and reduces survey fatigue
Drawbacks Some criticize its validity, reliability, and sensitivity

UMUX - Lite

(rewrite_ To improve the UMUX, Lewis et al. proposed a shorter all-positive questionnaire called the UMUX-Lite using the same 7-point scale with the following two items:

Statements:

  1. [This system’s] capabilities meet my requirements.
  2. [This system] is easy to use.

The UMUX-Lite also correlated highly with the SUS at r = .83 and the Net Promoter Score at r = .72, similar to our findings. Lewis et al. provided a regression equation to predict SUS scores from the two items (they call the UMUX-Liter) and found that the UMUX-Lite could predict SUS scores with about 99% accuracy. The regression equation is below

SUS Score = 0.65 ∗ ((UMUX-Lite Item1 + UMUX-Lite Item2 − 2) ∗ (100/12)) + 22.9

B2. Measuring Information Architecture

Findability

Users complete information-finding tasks in a representation of the navigation structure. These studies can be moderated or unmoderated.

  • First-click testing: Users click on a visual representation of the website to find information. This test can help identify issues with navigation placement and labeling.
  • Tree testing: Users navigate through a textual outline of the information architecture to complete tasks. This test can help evaluate the organization and findability of information.
  • Metrics:
  1. Task Success: Whether the user successfully found the information
  2. Task Time: How long it took the user to find the information
  3. Task Variability: Measures the different paths to achieve the same goal
  4. Task Difficulty: Perceived ease of use

Content Interpretation

  • Card Sort Test
  • Open: Group items into categories and label these categories. This surfaces the user's mental model for organizing content.
  • Closed: Same as above but users group items into given categories or buckets.

B3. Task Performance

These metrics are also a part of the summative SUS score but can be used to specifically investigate task-related user behaviors for improving UX.

  • Overall Task or Goal completion or success rate: percentage of tasks successfully completed
  • Total time on Task: Average time for accomplishing the tasks

B4. Measuring Cognitive Workload: NASA TLX

The NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) is a widely validated tool developed by NASA to assess subjective cognitive workload across six key dimensions:
Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration. Each is rated on a 0–100 scale using slider-based or Likert inputs, allowing users to reflect on the demands of a task.

NASA-TLX is especially effective in complex tasks where usability alone cannot explain user difficulty. It provides a composite score (average of six subscales) indicating the total cognitive burden experienced by users.

Dimensions Measured:

  1. Mental Demand: How much thinking, deciding, calculating, remembering, etc., was required? Was the task easy or demanding, simple or complex?
  2. Physical Demand: How much physical activity was required? Was the task easy or strenuous?
  3. Temporal Demand: How much time pressure did the user feel? Was the pace slow or rapid?
  4. Performance: How successful did users feel in accomplishing the goals of the task?
  5. Effort: How hard did users have to work to accomplish their level of performance?
  6. Frustration: How insecure, discouraged, irritated, stressed, and annoyed versus secure, gratified, content, relaxed, and complacent did the user feel?

Use in Research

NASA-TLX is particularly useful when evaluating redesigns or prototypes that aim to reduce cognitive strain, streamline workflows, or simplify decision-making. It complements usability metrics (e.g., SUS) by surfacing hidden burdens not captured by surface-level ease-of-use assessments.

Interpretation

  • TLX Score < 35: Low workload, high fluidity—task was minimally burdensome.
  • TLX Score 35–60: Moderate workload—some complexity, likely acceptable with minimal training.
  • TLX Score > 60: High workload—likely to produce user fatigue, confusion, or drop-off without support or redesign.

Example in Research

In our prototype tests, TLX scores were used to evaluate perceived effort and friction during core flows. The ChatBot and Foundations redesigns scored exceptionally low (TLX ~30–33), indicating smooth experiences. In contrast, the original Learning module and baseline flows exceeded TLX >60, pointing to significant usability and cognitive strain. These scores aligned with user feedback themes such as confusion, overwhelm, or need for external assistance.

For more: NASA TLX Reference - MeasuringU

[A] Attitudinal Metrics

aims to capture the attitudes, emotions, feelings of the users of our system.

A1. Measuring Satisfaction: CSAT

Customer Satisfaction Score: measures satisfaction of the experience CSAT is measured through customer feedback using a version of the following question.

  • How would you rate your overall satisfaction with the [goods/service] you received?
  • Respondents use the following 1 to 5 scale: Very unsatisfied, Unsatisfied, Neutral, Satisfied, Very satisfied.
  • Calculating CSAT: (Number of satisfied customers (4 and 5) / Number of survey responses) x 100 = % of satisfied customers

A2. Measuring Persuasion: NPS

Net Promoter Score: measures persuasion (source: Qualtrics) "NPS is a metric that tells you your overall customer experience quality, based on your existing customers’ likelihood to recommend your product, service, or organization. To do an NPS calculation, you’ll need to use the following Net Promoter Score (NPS) formula:

  • Total % of promoters – total % of detractors = net promoter score
  • The total % of people who would recommend your brand – the total % of those who wouldn’t = your net promoter score
  • Example: 70% promoters – 10% detractors = 60 is your NPS Score"

[IA] International Accessibility

IA1. Accessibility Audit

  • What it is: a structured evaluation of an experience to ensure it meets accessibility standards and is usable by people with disabilities.
  • How it is done: checks for compliance with guidelines such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) and assesses areas like keyboard navigation, screen reader compatibility, color contrast, and alternative text for images.
  • Goal: is to identify and address barriers that could prevent equal access for all users, improving usability and legal compliance.
