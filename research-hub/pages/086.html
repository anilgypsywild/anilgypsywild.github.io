🤔 What do we mean by a Data-Driven Persona in CVG's User-Centered Design Framework?

This section provides a clear, comparative, and evidence-based overview of the personas driving Compassion’s digital products and field partnerships. These tables were created to support rapid decision-making, cross-team clarity, and UX alignment across the Causes platform, Neighbor-to-Neighbor (N2N) fundraising, and the Community App for Frontline Church Partners (FCPs).

Unlike traditional personas that rely on stereotypes or fictional narratives, this system is built on twelve months of quantitative and qualitative research at CVG thereby integrating the following existing reports:

  1. MaxDiff motivational modeling from Quantifying Donor Motivations: MaxDiff Insights & Prospect Roadmap
  2. Prospect vs Donor Contrast Analysis from Contrast Report: Prospects vs Donor Motivations
  3. FCP role and workflow analysis from FCP Persona Survey Questions
  4. N2N Sandbox persona segmentation
  5. High Impact Donor research
  6. Persona Naming Survey
  7. Causes UX scorecards and usability benchmarks

Together, these research data-sources create a multi-layer persona architecture that recognizes:

  1. Supporter Personas – Observable behavioral roles across Causes and N2N
  2. Motivational Personas – Deep psychological drivers derived from MaxDiff analysis
  3. FCP Personas – Field-facing partners who power the Community App
  4. Generational Overlays – Distinct patterns for Next-Gen (18–34) and Current Gen (35+)

The tables below summarize these personas in a format optimized for fast reference, executive review, and product planning. They highlight differences in motivations, barriers, trust profiles, and UX implications, enabling teams to design experiences grounded in clear, quantitative truth rather than assumptions.

Persona A1: Prospect Supporter

First time giver on Compassion Causes

Snapshot

This persona is defined by a cognitive-first decision process, validated by the MaxDiff contrast showing prospects prioritize clarity, control, and simplicity far above emotional or faith cues.

They treat the first donation as a test of stewardship, not a relational commitment.

Role and Context

• Arrives from external channels (ads, SEO, referrals).
• Has low baseline trust in any nonprofit, including Compassion.
• Evaluates transparency and ease of giving before anything else.
• Not yet convinced of faith alignment; screens for responsible use of funds.

Motivations (Backed by MaxDiff Data)

Top motivators for prospects (ranked in the Deep Contrast Report):
• “I clearly understand where my money is going” (Score: 100)
• “I can make a tangible impact through my donation”

Prospects vs Donors (Key Differentators):
• Strong preference for autonomy

  • “I can choose how much I want to donate” (+3.6)
    • Strong preference for simplicity
  • “The donation process is simple and quick” (+6.0)
    • Strong preference for one time giving
  • “I have the option to give once” (+13.1)

Interpretation:
Prospects need clarity → trust → impact before they will accept emotional or faith-based framing.

Barriers & Risks

• Faith language before trust often backfires (−15.9 delta for prospects on faith alignment).
• Urgency without evidence feels manipulative (−9.5 delta).
• Complex checkout kills conversion.
• Generic “others donated” widgets have zero influence on prospects.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Reads details carefully; scans for evidence of stewardship.
• Prefers one time gifts under 50–100.
• Uses mobile heavily — friction is fatal.
• Ignores social proof; focuses on proof of use-of-funds.

Trust & Emotion Profile

According to the MaxDiff Insights Roadmap:
• Prospects are motivated by transparency and control, not faith or community.
• They need reassurance through simple, credible UI and evidence-based impact.

UX & Messaging Implications

Design the first donation as a safe, low-risk test.

• Lead with where the money goes, not “why this matters.”
• Show cost breakdowns and tangible before/after impact.
• Let users adjust amount freely; highlight one time options.
• Delay faith messaging until post-donation.
• Only use urgency when tied to documented, time-bound needs.
• Avoid unnecessary fields, long forms, or ambiguous CTAs.

Persona A2: Regular Supporter

Returning donor with early trust in Compassion

Snapshot

Once a prospect has a positive first experience, they begin shifting into the impact + trust + faith motivational cluster.
MaxDiff analysis confirms donors place far higher weight on emotion, faith alignment, and urgency than prospects.

This persona represents the backbone of Causes’ retention.

Role & Context

• Gives a few times per year.
• Comfortable exploring new causes.
• Responds to email re-engagement and in-app messaging.
• Starting to see Compassion as a trusted partner.

Motivations (Validated by MaxDiff)

From MaxDiff Insights Roadmap:
• Transparency stays #1 (for all donors).
• Tangible impact remains essential.
• Faith alignment rises sharply for returning donors (63.1 vs 47.2 for prospects).
• Personalization becomes more motivational (“I can see a specific project or child”).
• Urgency becomes more persuasive as trust grows.

Barriers & Risks

• If follow-up reporting is weak, trust decays.
• Generic emotional stories without continuity break connection.
• Too much urgency messaging reduces credibility.
• Repetitive or vague causes hurt motivation.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Skims cause descriptions, relying on organizational trust.
• More open to recurring giving or bundled causes.
• Looks for continuity: “my earlier gift → this new impact.”
• Emotion-oriented visuals and stories now have higher effect.

Trust & Emotion Profile

From Deep Contrast Report:
• Emotional drivers rise after trust is established.
• Faith alignment becomes a meaningful motivator, not a barrier.
• Gratitude messages still have minimal effect.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Highlight transparency → impact → faith connection.
• Use continuity narratives (“Your last gift helped…”).
• Introduce faith language gently but confidently.
• Provide clear updates and measurable outcomes.
• Use urgency cues when backed by specificity.
• Allow quick repeat-gift flows.

Persona A3: High Impact Donor (HID)

Large-gift donor with strategic and faith-driven motivations

Snapshot

This persona integrates donor-side MaxDiff data with your HID brief.
They exhibit the faith-aligned stewardship motivational pattern:
• High weight on faith alignment
• High dependence on trust in Compassion
• High need for personalization and multi-year visibility

They give fewer times, but in significantly higher amounts.

Role & Context

• Often works directly with staff or receives custom reports.
• Funds multi-year or large-impact projects.
• Treats giving as strategic Kingdom work.
• Expects reporting quality comparable to major charity partners or investment dashboards.

Motivations (Cited from MaxDiff + HID Research)

Top motivations:
• Transparency (absolute #1 for all donors)
• Impact at scale
• Faith alignment
• Specificity in project details (location, need, timeline)
• Trust in Compassion as a steward

Lowest motivators:
• Social proof (donor network support = near zero)
• Organizational thanks (very low utility)

These donors view giving as part of long-term calling, not an emotional impulse.

Barriers & Risks

• Missing, inconsistent, or late reporting
• Overly simple pages that don’t convey strategic seriousness
• Mixing large-gift pathways with small-gift consumer-like flows
• Lack of clarity about risk, governance, or the long-term plan

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Uses long-term dashboards, PDF reports, or personal meetings.
• Rarely influenced by emotional urgency but responds to evidence-based need.
• Not persuaded by social proof in any meaningful way.
• Less frequent but larger transactions.

Trust & Emotion Profile

From MaxDiff Insights Roadmap:
• Older and high-capacity donors place significantly more weight on faith alignment and trust.
• They require the deepest level of stewardship visibility — not just impact but continuity and governance credibility.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Provide high-level dashboards showing multi-year outcomes.
• Use mature, theological framing (stewardship, calling, faithfulness).
• Show project-level accountability and clarity of resource use.
• Avoid “small ask clutter” in flows designed for HID contexts.
• Reinforce long-term partnership continuity.

Persona B1: Influencer / Advocate

Mobilizer who activates an audience around Causes or Fundraisers

Snapshot

This persona represents creators, micro-influencers, youth leaders, and digital advocates who mobilize communities.
They display a Next-Gen motivational signature, placing high weight on:
• Urgency
• Emotional authenticity
• Visual impact
• Story-driven cues

They are essential for N2N peer-to-peer fundraising.

Role & Context

• Shares cause pages across TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, church groups.
• Wants quick, customizable fundraising flows.
• Motivated by being part of something larger than themselves.
• Acts as a multiplier, not just a giver.

Motivations (Backed by MaxDiff)

From MaxDiff Insights & Roadmap:
• Next-Gen users rank urgency significantly higher than Current-Gen.
• Emotional storytelling (+18% resonance) is a stronger driver for this persona.
• Visibility of progress matters more for younger donors.
• Impact clarity remains foundational.

From Deep Contrast Report:
• Prospects (a large part of influencer audiences) place high weight on simplicity and autonomy when giving through links.

Barriers & Risks

• Any friction in sharing links reduces engagement sharply.
• If impact visuals are weak or unclear, audience engagement drops.
• If progress feels “invisible,” their community loses momentum.
• Overly corporate or non-authentic messaging harms trust.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Creates short-form content tied to cause moments.
• Shares rapid updates, prefers real-time results.
• Prefers mobile-first flows that are fast and responsive.
• Less influenced by faith-aligned framing; more by emotion and immediacy.

Trust & Emotion Profile

From MaxDiff Insights:
• Younger donors value authentic emotional storytelling more than stability or institutional narratives.
• They trust influencers over institutions, so platform trust is mediated through the influencer.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Prioritize instant shareability (copy link, social share buttons, QR codes).
• Provide real-time donation updates.
• Offer simple auto-generated scripts, reels, and image templates.
• Keep cause pages visual-first, story-first, impact-first.
• Avoid long text blocks or heavy theological framing on initial screens.

Persona B2: College Mobilizer

Next-Gen student leader coordinating collective fundraising

Snapshot

This persona is a hyper-Next-Gen segment with the strongest emotional + urgency signature of all supporter personas.
They score very high on urgency, emotional authenticity, and impact visuals, per age-based MaxDiff segmentation.

They are highly social, competitive, and driven by community belonging.

Role & Context

• Leads college groups, clubs, ministries, campus teams.
• Uses fundraisers as team-building or challenge-based events.
• Converts peers, not families; the network is tight-knit.

Motivations (Validated by MaxDiff)

• Next-Gen donors overweight urgency and emotional stories.
• Impact visualization matters significantly more than for older donors.
• Simplicity of giving (prospect signature) is key for new participants.

This persona is a hybrid: urgent + emotional + clarity-first.

Barriers & Risks

• Slow or complex flows kill momentum.
• Non-mobile-friendly design blocks participation.
• Lack of progress tracking lowers group enthusiasm.
• Overly formal voice alienates students.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Runs time-bound campaigns (“Let’s raise $500 by midnight”).
• Shares frequently across social platforms and group chats.
• Motivated by leaderboard-like competitive dynamics.
• Posts transformation photos and short emotional captions.

Trust & Emotion Profile

MaxDiff analysis:
• Younger donors are emotion-oriented and immediacy-driven.
• They engage emotionally first, intellectually second.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Add real-time progress visuals (thermometer, percent funded).
• Provide “challenge mode” tools for group goals.
• Keep all flows under 15 seconds to start donating.
• Use emotional visuals paired with simple impact statements.
• Tone should be encouraging, social, energetic.

Persona B3: Supporting Church / Organized Faith Community

Congregational or group-based fundraisers

Snapshot

This persona fuses both Current-Gen (leaders) and Next-Gen (participants).

Leaders:
• Motivated by faith alignment and stewardship.
• Motivated by long-term trust in Compassion.

Younger members:
• Motivated by urgency, emotion, and community belonging.

They raise money collectively and need tools to coordinate groups.

Role & Context

• Fundraising tied to sermons, youth ministry, small groups.
• Needs centralized tracking for group totals.
• Prefers stewardship language (“We are giving as a church”).

Motivations (Supported by MaxDiff & N2N)

From MaxDiff Roadmap:
• Current-Gen donors overweight faith alignment and trust.
• Next-Gen participants overweight urgency + authenticity.

From N2N research:
• Churches need multi-user tracking, updates, and shareable content.

Barriers & Risks

• Tools not built for collective goals.
• Hard-to-track totals across many donors.
• Not enough visible connection between church giving and real impact.
• Inconsistent updates produce skepticism.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Leaders communicate via email or shared links.
• Younger members share via social media stories and group chats.
• Churches commit to multi-week campaigns.
• Prefer clarity + trust + spiritual framing that aligns with Christian mission.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Current-Gen leaders anchor trust in Compassion’s stewardship record.
• Younger members anchor trust in emotional resonance and community participation.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Create “group fundraising mode” with team dashboards.
• Provide sermon-ready materials, slides, and impact stories.
• Show faith-aligned messaging for leaders.
• Show visual transformation and urgency cues for younger participants.
• Provide automated impact updates for the whole group.

Persona B4: Social Circle Fundraiser

Individual raising funds from friends and family

Snapshot

This persona appears frequently in N2N use cases: individuals raising money around birthdays, runs, life events, or personal passions.
They borrow traits from both Prospects and Influencers.

Critically, MaxDiff shows this persona’s audience is:
• Highly motivated by clarity + impact
• Not motivated by social proof (near zero utility)

Meaning:
“My friends donated” is not a strong motivator — clarity is.

Role & Context

• Creates a one-off fundraiser to rally friends or family.
• Wants effortless page creation.
• Shares links rapidly across text, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook.

Motivations (Backed by MaxDiff)

• Transparency and impact are dominant motivators.
• Simplicity in giving flow matters for their network.
• Emotional visuals increase share rates.
• Faith alignment may or may not be relevant depending on their circle.

Barriers & Risks

• If setup takes too long, they abandon the idea.
• If messaging feels corporate, friends won’t click.
• If progress is not shown clearly, motivation drops.
• Gratitude messages have almost no effect on giving.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Shares multiple times in short bursts.
• Uses personal stories, selfies, or emotional captions.
• Often uses mobile-only.
• Fundraising window is short (24–96 hours).

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Their audience is a mix of prospects and young donors.
• Emotional cues help, but clarity converts.
• Their own trust in Compassion is based on ease-of-use and visible impact.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Provide ultra-fast fundraiser creation tools (60 seconds or less).
• Use highly visual templates.
• Highlight clear impact statements at the top.
• Make the progress bar more visually exciting than the basic version.
• Provide pre-written messages for sharing.
• Delay theological or heavy storytelling until later.

FCP PERSONA 1: Project Director / FCP Leader

Oversees Compassion’s field implementation at the church level

Snapshot

This persona is the primary administrative leader within an FCP. They are responsible for program oversight, staff coordination, ensuring data accuracy, and reporting. They experience heavy operational load and rely on the Community App to reduce administrative friction.

Role & Context

• Leads the entire Compassion partnership at their church.
• Ensures compliance, reporting, child safety, and documentation.
• Often works in low-bandwidth, high-constraint environments.
• Accountability to pastors, staff, and Compassion field offices.

Motivations (Supported by FCP Survey Questions)

• Wants a simple and reliable workflow for task submission.
• Values accuracy, timeliness, and reduced duplication.
• Motivated by clarity in expectations (“What exactly does Compassion need from us?”).

Barriers & Pain Points

• Slow or failing uploads (especially photos and progress reports).
• Confusing form fields or unclear instructions.
• Repetition (“Why do I enter the same info twice?”).
• Lack of visibility into what has been submitted vs what is still pending.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Frequently multitasks: staff supervision + administration + ministry work.
• Uses both mobile and desktop depending on electricity/internet conditions.
• Relies heavily on WhatsApp/SMS for communication.
• Prefers tools that allow offline work or saved drafts.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Trust is built through predictability, not aesthetics.
• Frustration spikes when the app “loses work” or crashes.
• They value partnership language—they see themselves as co-laborers in mission.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Provide clear task lists with status indicators (“Due,” “Submitted,” “Needs Correction”).
• Allow drafts and offline saving.
• Minimize field duplication.
• Include simple how-to guides and embedded help icons.
• Design for low bandwidth; compress images automatically.
• Clarify which tasks are required vs optional.

FCP PERSONA 2: Health Worker / Child Wellbeing Staff

Responsible for capturing health, nutrition, and wellbeing data for children

Snapshot

This persona performs physical checkups, home visits, wellness assessments, and submits health-related updates and photos. Their work environment is often mobile, rural, and low-connectivity.

Role & Context

• Travels between homes, clinics, and church compounds.
• Takes photos of children, meals, activities, or interventions.
• Needs to log information accurately and quickly.

Motivations (Validated by FCP Survey)

• Wants a fast, dependable system for uploading wellness updates.
• Motivated by child safety and accuracy in reporting.
• Needs clarity: “What photo counts as acceptable documentation?”

Barriers & Pain Points

• Image upload failures are the #1 frustration point.
• Unclear error messages (“Upload failed”—but why?).
• Large forms that require scrolling or typing lengthy notes on mobile.
• When the app times out or loses signal, they lose unsaved entries.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Mostly mobile-first user.
• Works in low-connectivity zones frequently.
• Takes photos using the device camera, then uploads in batches when signal returns.
• Writes short, to-the-point comments rather than long narratives.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Emotional driver is child safety, dignity, and stewardship of the child’s story.
• Trust is broken when tech failure jeopardizes accuracy or timeliness.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Build a super-lightweight photo upload pipeline (auto compression, retry in background).
• Enable offline capture of photos + notes with sync later.
• Provide checklists for “What photos Compassion needs.”
• Ensure form fields are short, mobile-friendly, and scannable.
• Clear error messages (“File too large—compressing now”).

FCP PERSONA 3: Educator / Child Development Worker

Provides educational, spiritual, and social development activities for children

Snapshot

This persona focuses on child development activities such as tutoring, lessons, devotions, life skills, and mentoring. They are responsible for reporting activity-level evidence, photos, and simple narratives.

Role & Context

• Runs classes and group sessions.
• Documents attendance and participation.
• Needs structured guidance on what counts as quality evidence.

Motivations (From FCP Survey)

• Wants to show meaningful child development outcomes.
• Motivated by storytelling that honors the child.
• Needs clear, structured prompts (“Write 1–2 sentences about the session”).

Barriers & Pain Points

• Free-text fields without examples cause uncertainty (“Am I writing the right thing?”).
• Many educators have minimal typing skills.
• Repeated narrative entries across tasks create fatigue.
• Confusion over which sessions require photos.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Often uses older Android devices.
• Takes photos in classrooms or outdoor spaces with poor lighting.
• Writes brief summaries, not long paragraphs.
• Relies on templates and pre-written phrases offline.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Wants to feel competent and supported, not judged.
• Fear: “My submission will be rejected because I didn’t write enough.”

UX & Messaging Implications

• Use “micro-prompts” for narrative entries (“Describe what the children learned today”).
• Provide writing examples and templates.
• Clarify when photos are required vs optional.
• Offer quick-tap tags (“Bible Study”, “Math Lesson”, “Life Skills”, etc.).
• Keep camera workflow extremely simple.

FCP PERSONA 4: Accountant / Administrative Assistant

Steward of financial and operational accuracy at the FCP

Snapshot

This persona handles budgets, receipts, financial uploads, and administrative documentation. They care deeply about accuracy, auditability, and clarity, and have low tolerance for ambiguous flows.

Role & Context

• Manages receipts, approvals, procurement, and documentation.
• Tracks compliance tasks and deadlines.
• Ensures correct financial reporting to Compassion.

Motivations (From FCP Survey)

• Clear and traceable upload flows.
• Certainty that documents have been accepted.
• Ability to review submissions easily.
• Low-error, low-ambiguity processes.

Barriers & Pain Points

• Confusing or inconsistent naming of upload fields.
• Difficulty tracking what has been approved vs pending.
• Poor scanning/photo quality leads to rejected receipts.
• No centralized summary of financial submissions.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Often uses desktop computers or shared devices at the church.
• Checks tasks at the end of the week rather than in real time.
• Uploads receipts in batches.
• Saves copies of everything offline as a protection measure.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Trust = clarity, predictability, accuracy.
• Anxiety around making mistakes that delay funding or cause compliance risk.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Provide a financial “summary dashboard” with status indicators.
• Standardize naming conventions and field labels.
• Enable document preview before submission.
• Auto-scan or auto-enhance receipt images.
• Show confirmation states explicitly (“Approved”, “Under Review”).

FCP PERSONA 5: Pastor / Church Leader

Spiritual and organizational head of the church overseeing the Compassion partnership

Snapshot

This persona is the vision carrier and spiritual authority at the FCP. Their motivation is rooted in stewardship, pastoral care, and honoring the partnership. Their role is less operational and more relational and strategic.

Role & Context

• Supports staff, motivates volunteers.
• Ensures spiritual integrity and theological alignment.
• Communicates the mission of the program to the congregation.

Motivations (From FCP Survey)

• Stewardship of children and the community.
• Confidence that Compassion is a trustworthy and aligned partner.
• Clear understanding of program impact on children.
• High value on transparency and relational warmth.

Barriers & Pain Points

• Too much administrative burden placed on staff.
• Lack of clarity around expectations for program outcomes.
• Confusing or inconsistent communication from Compassion office.
• Difficulty seeing the “big picture” from fragmented updates.

Behaviors & Digital Habits

• Does not always use the app directly but influences staff adoption.
• Engages with summary reports or presentations from directors.
• Uses WhatsApp or SMS to communicate pastoral messages.
• Shares success stories with the congregation when confident in accuracy.

Trust & Emotion Profile

• Trust rooted in shared faith, mission alignment, and long-term relationship.
• Wants assurance that Compassion honors its commitments.
• Needs clarity about how the program improves children’s lives spiritually, educationally, and socially.

UX & Messaging Implications

• Provide a high-level “impact overview” or monthly summary for leaders.
• Ensure communication is pastoral, relational, and respectful.
• Reduce staff burden by simplifying admin tasks.
• Offer ready-to-share stories and photos for sermons or small groups.
• Avoid jargon; use warm, ministry-aligned language.
