🗒️ Heuristic Review: Compassion Causes Category & Discovery Flows

Purpose
Support Design and Product prioritization for improving cause discoverability, clarity, and donor confidence.

Artifacts Reviewed

  • Categories overview and footer taxonomy
  • Category landing pages for Water, Technology, Education, Health, Income Generation, Infrastructure
  • Cause card grids and Proof of Impact sections

Primary sources

  • Categories overview and structure
  • Technology category page
  • Water category page

Evaluation framework: Ben Shneiderman’s Golden Rules of Interface Design

Research Summary

The current Causes category experience is visually consistent but conceptually misaligned with donor mental models.

The dominant issue is category ambiguity, especially in categories organized by means or implementation(Technology, Infrastructure), rather than donor relevant outcomes.

This creates:
• Slower discovery
• Hesitation and second guessing
• Over reliance on scrolling and search
• Reduced emotional momentum toward donation

Water performs best because it naturally aligns means and outcome.
Technology performs worst because it bundles unrelated outcomes under an abstract mechanism.

Key Heuristic Findings and Impact

Finding 1: Conceptual Consistency Is Broken

Evidence
In the Technology category, causes include laptops for education, prosthetics for health, and innovation labs for community use .

User Impact
Users must mentally translate
What is this category? → What does this actually help?

Design Risk
High cognitive load before emotional engagement.

Finding 2: Categories Explain Compassion’s Structure, Not Donor Outcomes

Evidence
Category labels like Technology and Infrastructure describe how help is delivered, not what changes for a child or family.

User Impact
New or casual donors struggle to answer -- Is this what I want to support?

Product Risk
Delayed decision making and drop off during browsing.

Finding 3: Feedback and Boundaries Are Missing

Evidence
Category landing pages do not explain what types of causes belong there or how overlap is handled .

User Impact
Users hesitate and backtrack due to fear of choosing the wrong category.

Design Risk
Exploration feels risky rather than encouraged.

Finding 4: Memory Load Is Unnecessarily High

Evidence
Once inside a category, users are not reminded why causes belong there.

User Impact
Users must remember category logic across screens.

Product Risk
Lower discoverability and weaker cause comparison.

Design and Product Implications

What Design May Prioritize

  1. Shift category framing toward outcomes first language
  2. Reduce ambiguity by:
    • Adding clarifying microcopy on category pages
    • Surfacing examples of what belongs inside
  3. Introduce tags or secondary labels to handle overlap without forcing exclusivity

What Product May Prioritize

  1. Treat category confusion as a conversion risk, not just IA debt
  2. Support multiple exploration paths
    • Outcome based
    • Urgency based
    • Beneficiary based
  3. Preserve user context when switching categories

Tldr;

Based on the actual category implementations reviewed:

  1. The Causes IA currently reflects internal program logic more than donor decision logic.
    1. Water succeeds because it unintentionally aligns both.
    2. Technology and Infrastructure fail because they do not.

This creates a clear, research backed case for outcome first categorization, supported by card sorting and concept testing before IA lock.

🏅 Ben Shneiderman’s Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design

Source: Ben Shneiderman, Designing the User Interface
Referenced in internal review to ground heuristic evaluation.

Rule 1: Strive for Consistency

Interfaces should maintain consistency in:
• Terminology
• Layout
• Visual hierarchy
• Interaction patterns

Why it matters
Consistency reduces learning time and prevents user confusion by allowing patterns to be reused mentally.

Rule 2: Seek Universal Usability

Interfaces should accommodate:
• Novice and expert users
• Different levels of domain knowledge
• Diverse abilities and contexts

This includes progressive disclosure and flexibility without overwhelming users.

Rule 3: Offer Informative Feedback

Every user action should receive:
• Immediate
• Meaningful
• Appropriate feedback

Feedback helps users understand system state and the result of their actions.

Rule 4: Design Dialogs to Yield Closure

Interactions should be structured into:
• Clear beginnings
• Middle stages
• Meaningful endings

Users should feel a sense of completion after key steps.

Rule 5: Prevent Errors

Good design:
• Anticipates common user mistakes
• Makes errors difficult or impossible
• Guides users toward correct actions

Prevention is preferred over error messages.

Rule 6: Permit Easy Reversal of Actions

Interfaces should support:
• Undo
• Back
• Cancel

Reversibility reduces anxiety and encourages exploration.

Rule 7: Support Internal Locus of Control

Users should feel:
• In control of the interface
• That the system responds to them
• Not dictated or constrained unnecessarily

The system should follow the user’s intent, not override it.

Rule 8: Reduce Short Term Memory Load

Interfaces should:
• Minimize reliance on memory
• Make options and distinctions visible
• Avoid forcing users to recall information across screens

Recognition should be favored over recall.
