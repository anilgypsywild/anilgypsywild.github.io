⚙️ Collaborating on User Research Studies with ART Form

This tutorial is designed to help designers understand how to create user research study requests, write testable hypotheses, and follow the research process

1. Creating a User Research Study Request

A well-crafted research study request is essential for clear communication and successful outcomes. Please follow these steps:

Microsoft form is here: [reference omitted]

Step 1: Define the Problem

  • Clearly articulate the issue you’re addressing.
    • Example: “Users seem to have difficulty understanding the impact of their donations through our app.”

Step 2: Set Objectives

  • Specify what you want to learn from the study.
    • Example: “Determine which timeline design (horizontal or vertical) is easier for users to understand and navigate.”

Step 3: Write Testable Hypotheses

A testable hypothesis predicts the outcome of your research in measurable terms. Use this formula:

If [design change/feature] is implemented, then [expected outcome] will , because [reason].

Examples:

  • If we use a vertical timeline, then users will locate updates faster because the design aligns with natural scrolling behaviors.
  • If users see key updates immediately, then they will report higher satisfaction because it minimizes interaction effort.

Step 4: Identify Metrics

Specify how you’ll measure success:

  • Quantitative: Usability scores (e.g., SUS, UMUX-Lite), time-on-task, or error rates.
  • Qualitative: User quotes, feedback themes, or observed behavior patterns.

2. Phases of the Research Process

Designers need to align with researchers on these key phases:

Phase 1: Intake

  1. Submit the Request
    • Include: Problem, objectives, hypotheses, and metrics.
  2. Collaborate on Scope
    • Discuss constraints (e.g., budget, time, recruitment).
  3. Finalize the Plan
    • Agree on the research method (e.g., A/B test, survey, interviews).

Phase 2: Planning

  1. Define Study Tasks
    • Design realistic user scenarios/tasks.
    • Example: “Locate the section of the timeline showing ‘12 youth baptized on Dec 17.’”
  2. Create Prototypes
    • Prepare the design variations for testing.
    • Example: Low-fidelity vertical and horizontal timelines.
  3. Recruit Participants
    • Define criteria (e.g., Christian donors with prior giving experience).
    • Use tools like UserZoom or panel recruitment services.

Phase 3: Execution

  1. Pre-Test Surveys
    • Gather baseline data about user familiarity or expectations.
    • Example: “How familiar are you with Compassion’s platforms?”
  2. Conduct the Study
    • Ensure clear instructions for participants.
    • Example: “Interact with both timeline designs and locate specific updates.”
  3. Capture Data
    • Quantitative: Heatmaps, time-on-task.
    • Qualitative: User feedback on preferences.

Phase 4: Sharing

  1. Analyze Results
    • Summarize key insights.
    • Example: “85% of users preferred the vertical timeline due to its intuitive flow.”
  2. Prepare a Report
    • Include metrics, participant feedback, and recommendations.
    • Use visuals like heatmaps and charts for clarity.
  3. Share with Stakeholders
    • Present findings in a concise, actionable format.
    • Example: “We recommend implementing the vertical timeline due to its superior usability and preference.”

Tips for Designers

  • Be Specific: Clearly define what you need to learn and how it impacts design decisions.
  • Think Like a User: Focus on the user's perspective, not just the design elements.
  • Collaborate Early: Engage researchers during the planning phase to refine your hypotheses and tasks.
  • Embrace Feedback: Use research results to iterate on designs, even if they challenge initial assumptions.
