🔙 Backtracking as a Behavioral Signal of Usability Breakdown

  • Backtracking behavior is not just navigation noise.
  • In usability research, it can function as an observable indicator of:
    1. interaction breakdown
    2. uncertainty
    3. mismatch between user expectation and system behavior
    4. or, failed progress toward a goal.
  • David Akers’ Stanford dissertation formalized this as backtracking analysis, showing that backtracking events such as undo, erase, and abort can be logged and used to identify usability problems in software workflows.
    • In Stanford HCI’s 2009 study with Google SketchUp, undo and erase episodes together revealed over 90 percent of the problems rated as severe, including some that self report alone did not capture. (HCI Group)

For Causes, the equivalent insight is:

When users

  • reverse
  • revisit
  • abandon
  • or loop

within the flow, they may be signaling

  • confusion
  • low trust,
  • weak information scent
  • or decision uncertainty.

This makes backtracking especially valuable for Causes because the workflow is not just navigational. It is also evaluative, emotional, and financially consequential.

Conceptual Definition

Backtracking

Backtracking refers to observable reversal or recovery behavior after a user encounters friction, uncertainty, or a mismatch between expectation and interface response.

In creation oriented applications, Akers defines backtracking through operations such as undo, erase, and abort. In Causes, we generalize the same idea to decision and navigation flows. (HCI Group)

☕ Causes Specific Interpretation

Within the Causes workflow, backtracking can be treated as a behavioral indicator, not a direct diagnosis.

It does not automatically mean failure, but it is a strong candidate signal that something in the user experience warrants investigation.

Backtracking in Causes may indicate:

  1. low clarity
  2. low confidence
  3. low trust
  4. missing proof
  5. weak relevance
  6. unresolved comparison behavior
  7. disrupted momentum toward donation
  8. ....

💠 Causes Specific Forms of Backtracking

🗺️ 1. Navigation Backtracking

Examples:

  1. Cause detail → Cause list
  2. Cart → Cause detail
  3. Checkout step 2 → Checkout step 1
  4. Browser back after entering a cause

Interpretation:
The user may be reconsidering the choice, recovering from a wrong path, or looking for information they expected but did not find.

🧠 2. Cognitive Backtracking

Examples:

  1. scrolling down, then repeatedly back up to earlier content
  2. re reading story, cost, or impact language
  3. revisiting the donation amount or details section multiple times

Interpretation:
The user may be trying to resolve ambiguity, re validate meaning, or compare competing cues.

💵 3. Decision Backtracking

Examples:

  1. add to cart → remove from cart
  2. begin checkout → abandon
  3. select a cause → leave and compare another cause
  4. initiate donation → stop before payment step

Interpretation:
The user may not yet trust the cause, may not understand the value proposition, or may not feel ready to commit.

⏳ 4. Micro Backtracking

Examples:

  1. repeated clicking on the same element
  2. hesitation before CTA
  3. hover, pause, then move away
  4. switching between tabs or panels before acting

Interpretation:
These are smaller but still meaningful signals of indecision, uncertainty, or poor information scent.

🤔 Why this belongs in the Metrics Framework

Backtracking gives us a bridge between:

  1. qualitative interpretation
  2. prototype instrumentation
  3. production analytics
  4. future personalization

🧯 This matters because

  1. Unanchored self-report alone is weak.
    • A rating like “trust = 3” has little meaning unless paired with behavioral evidence and predefined anchors.
  2. Backtracking adds observable signal to the measurement system and helps operationalize constructs such as:
    • Trust, Hesitation, and Clarity.
  3. Akers’ dissertation specifically argues that backtracking events can be synchronized with contextual data such as screen capture to better characterize usability problems. (HCI Group)

Appendix A1: Operational Metrics for Causes

🛹 Core Backtracking Metrics

1. Backtrack Rate

Number of backtracking events per session.

Example:

  • number of backward navigations
  • number of cart removals
  • number of return to prior screens

2. Backtrack Depth

How far back the user moves in the workflow.

Example:

  • checkout → cart = shallow backtrack
  • checkout → cause detail = deeper backtrack
  • cause detail → homepage = deepest reversal

Interpretation:
Greater depth may indicate more serious workflow breakdown or greater uncertainty.

3. Backtrack Frequency

How often the same user reverses direction during one session.

Interpretation:
Repeated reversals may indicate unresolved confusion or a comparison behavior not well supported by the interface.

4. Time to Backtrack

Elapsed time between entering a step and reversing from it.

Interpretation:
Very short time to backtrack may indicate immediate mismatch or surprise. Longer time before backtracking may indicate delayed doubt after reading or evaluating content.

5. Backtrack Context

Where the backtracking occurs.

Recommended contexts:

  • discovery
  • cause list
  • cause detail
  • cart
  • checkout
  • payment

Interpretation:
Backtracking is more meaningful when tied to location in the funnel.

6. Loop Count

Number of repeated sequences such as:

  • list → detail → list
  • detail → cart → detail
  • cart → checkout → cart

Interpretation:
Loops can indicate comparison behavior, unresolved trust, or missing decision support.

💟 Appendix A2: Mapping Backtracking to Behavioral Constructs

Construct Potential Backtracking Signal Interpretation
Trust detail viewed, then immediate exit or reversal user lacks confidence or proof
Hesitation repeated pauses and reversals before CTA user is uncertain about next step
Clarity wrong path followed by immediate return user misunderstood label or flow
Relevance repeated comparison across causes user does not see a sufficiently good fit
Motivation add to cart then remove intent is unstable or emotionally weak

🖌️ Appendix A3: Prototype Study Implications

Backtracking can be observed in prototype studies if the prototype supports path instrumentation, first click capture, task flow tracking, and time stamps.

Recommended prototype task framing

“Please explore this cause and proceed as if you were deciding whether to support it.”

Log the following:

  • first click
  • step sequence
  • backward navigations
  • time between steps
  • repeat visits to sections
  • abandoned actions
  • verbalized doubt during think aloud or retrospective review

Important caution

Backtracking is not a standalone verdict. It should be interpreted alongside:

  • first click accuracy
  • comprehension
  • clarity score
  • trust score
  • qualitative explanation

This is consistent with Stanford’s framing that backtracking analysis is a complement to, not a replacement for, other usability evaluation methods. (HCI Group)

🏭 Appendix A4: Production Analytics Implications

For live Causes analytics, backtracking can be captured through event streams and session paths.

Recommended event examples

  • view_cause_list
  • view_cause_detail
  • return_to_list
  • add_to_cart
  • remove_from_cart
  • begin_checkout
  • checkout_back

Derived live metrics

  • backtrack_rate_by_segment
  • backtrack_rate_by_entry_point
  • backtrack_depth_distribution
  • abandon_after_backtrack
  • repeat_compare_loops
  • checkout_reversal_rate

These should be analyzed by:

  • device
  • source channel
  • user cohort
  • cause category
  • new versus returning visitor

🎨 Appendix A5: Design and Product Use

Backtracking is a candidate signal of friction, ambiguity, or unresolved evaluation.

That gives the team a practical way to investigate:

  • what information was missing
  • where trust was not sufficiently established
  • where the narrative failed to support commitment
  • whether the system supports comparison and reflection appropriately

🙊 Appendix A6: Suggested language for cross team use

  1. This is a general behavioral measurement approach, not a Causes only concept.
  2. The same logic can be extended to Community and other Compassion workflows where users move through meaning making, decision making, or action flows.
  3. In that sense, backtracking is part of a broader measurement framework for behavioral UX signals.

Appendix B: Backtracking Analysis for Causes

B.1 Definition

Backtracking analysis is the study of reversal, recovery, and re navigation behaviors as indicators of usability friction, uncertainty, or workflow mismatch.

The original Stanford work focused on undo, erase, and abort operations in creation oriented software; in Causes, the same logic can be adapted to navigational and decision flows. (HCI Group)

🧩 B.2 Causes Workflow Mapping

Causes Step Example Backtracking Event Possible Meaning
Homepage homepage → cause list → homepage weak information scent or lack of relevance
Cause List cause list → detail → cause list comparison, uncertainty, insufficient fit
Cause Detail detail → list or homepage low trust, low clarity, weak story
Cart add → remove unstable commitment
Checkout begin checkout → back to cart unresolved doubt or missing information
Payment payment view → exit final stage uncertainty or concern

⚕️ B.3 Severity Heuristic

Not all backtracking events are equal. Recommended severity rules:

Low severity

  • one reversal with short recovery

Medium severity

  • repeated list/detail loops
  • pause followed by reversal
  • cart removal after extended reading

High severity

  • deep checkout reversal
  • repeated checkout exits
  • multiple loops without progress
  • abandonment immediately after key trust or impact content

💟 B.4 Relation to Trust, Hesitation, and Clarity

Backtracking should be treated as one of the clearest behavioral manifestations of hesitation and can also act as a downstream signal of low trust or low clarity.

Examples

  • Low trust: detail page viewed, proof ignored, user exits
  • Low clarity: wrong click followed by immediate reversal
  • High hesitation: pause, reread, backtrack, then delay again

🚅 B.5 Proposed Metrics for Causal ML Pipeline

Candidate features to track and log

  1. backtrack_rate
  2. backtrack_depth
  3. loop_count
  4. time_to_backtrack
  5. abandon_after_backtrack
  6. cart_remove_rate
  7. compare_loop_indicator
  8. revisit_same_cause_indicator

Why these matter

These features can improve:

  1. propensity prediction
  2. friction detection
  3. segment discovery
  4. personalization policy design

:people_holding_hands_dark_skin_tone_light_skin_tone: B.6 Personalization Implications

If users exhibit:

  1. repeated comparison loops → show stronger differentiation or fit signals
  2. cart removal → surface reassurance or impact specificity
  3. shallow engagement then reversal → increase proof and trust elements earlier
  4. checkout reversal → address missing confidence signals before payment

This connects directly to the Causes personalization roadmap because it shifts the measurement system from static self report to dynamic behavioral inference.

📓 B.7 Research Recommendation

  1. Immediate recommendation: Include backtracking as a predefined behavioral metric in prototype studies and in future live instrumentation.
  2. Short term recommendation: Review prototype and live pathing together so that qualitative meaning and event level traces can be interpreted jointly
  3. Longer term recommendation: Use backtracking features as inputs to segmentation and causal modeling rather than treating them as isolated usability artifacts.
