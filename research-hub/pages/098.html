Research strategy

Choose the method for the uncertainty

Qualitative and quantitative methods answer complementary questions. Choose the evidence needed for the decision, then design the study.

  1. 1Frame

    What decision must the team make?

  2. 2Identify uncertainty

    What do we need to understand or estimate?

  3. 3Select evidence

    Choose methods, participants, and measures.

  4. 4Integrate

    Explain what the combined evidence supports.

Two approaches, complementary strengths

Qualitative

Understand context and mechanisms

Investigate how people interpret an experience, where difficulties arise, and why a need matters.

Examples: contextual inquiry, interviews, diary studies, and diagnostic usability sessions.

Output: explanations, themes, observed breakdowns, and hypotheses with supporting evidence.

Quantitative

Estimate magnitude and compare outcomes

Estimate prevalence, performance, relative priority, or differences under a defined study design.

Examples: surveys, benchmarks, experiments, analytics, and choice models.

Output: estimates, distributions, effects, and uncertainty.

Neither approach is inherently faster or more rigorous. Sample size, recruitment, instrument quality, analysis, and the question determine what a study can support.

Match the question to a method

UncertaintyUseful methodsDecision supported
What goals and constraints shape the work?Contextual inquiry, interviews, field observation, diaries.Define the problem and relevant use scenarios.
Can people find and understand the content?Card sorting, tree testing, comprehension tasks.Refine taxonomy, navigation, and terminology.
Can people complete a workflow?Moderated or unmoderated usability evaluation; task metrics.Identify breakdowns and evaluate revisions.
Which needs or features take priority?Qualitative needs discovery followed by MaxDiff or other suitable preference methods.Make explicit tradeoffs rather than rely on isolated importance ratings.
How does the current experience perform?Representative task benchmarks, validated questionnaires, analytics.Establish a baseline and identify the next investigation.
Does a change improve an outcome?A controlled experiment or a defensible comparative evaluation.Estimate an effect while accounting for plausible alternatives.
Does value persist over time?Longitudinal research, cohort analysis, follow-up interviews.Distinguish initial enthusiasm from sustained use.

Combine methods with a purpose

A usability baseline can locate a performance problem; interviews and observations can explain it; card sorting can investigate the organization of content; a comparative test can evaluate the proposed revision. The sequence follows the uncertainty, not a fixed checklist.
