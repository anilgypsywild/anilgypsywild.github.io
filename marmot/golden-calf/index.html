From The Marmot Ate Beans

The Golden Calf Hummed at Seven Hundred Watts

The microwave became the center of camp life faster than I expected. This should not have surprised me.

Human beings worship whatever converts waiting into appetite.

At Stewart, the microwave stood there humming its little industrial hymn while men orbited it with ramen, beans, rice, cheese, tortillas, coffee, plastic bowls, improvised recipes, and the gloomy sacerdotal seriousness of prisoners who had discovered that flavor was one of the few freedoms still smuggled legally into the institution.

The Golden Calf has been upgraded.

I did not check the label. I am a scientist, but even I have limits.

Still, the theology was obvious. Men gathered around it. Men waited for it. Men fought over time before it. Men stared into its rotating light as if their soup, beans, and dignity were being transfigured behind the little perforated window.

In Exodus, they built an idol from gold. At Stewart, commissary wealth came in foil packets, sodium bricks, instant noodles, coffee packets, sugar, spice, tortillas, tuna, and whatever else could survive institutional storage.

The altar hummed. The faithful queued.

I was not above it. Let me not pretend. Ramen had already rescued me from despair more than once. Beans too. Prison food occupied a strange moral category: edible enough to keep the State from being accused of starvation, bad enough to make commissary feel like civilization, and repetitive enough to make one packet of seasoning taste like a private revolution.

A man who, outside, might have judged instant Top Ramen noodles as chemically flavored bachelor sadness could, inside, regard them as infrastructure, fool!

I learned this at NNCC from Jerry. Hot water. Ramen. Beans. Save what can be saved. Trade what can be traded. Do not eat everything at once. The store comes every other week. Plan. Prison cuisine is not cooking exactly: It is inventory management under culinary duress.

At camp, the microwave expanded the empire.

Men had methods. Men had systems. Men knew how long to heat, when to stir, when to cover, when to let something sit, how much water to add, how to thicken beans, how to stretch cheese, how to make ramen behave less like punishment and more like food.

If you listened carefully, you could hear the oral tradition forming. No cookbook. No measurements. Just a prison apprenticeship. A man would lean over another man’s bowl and say, “Nah, not yet. Put it back.”

Another would shake his head as if witnessing sacrilege. “Too much water, bro.”

A third would stand there with his arms crossed, guarding his noodles like a medieval monk preserving illuminated manuscripts from barbarian invasion.

This is how civilization begins again after collapsing. Not with constitutions. With fucking soup and beans.

M understood the microwave economy immediately because M understood its appetite. He understood that hungry men could be organized. He understood that men with spice, coffee, ramen, beans, sugar, and favors could create small kingdoms inside large cages. He could draw men toward him the way sugar draws ants, except ants have more dignity and usually less debt.

Stretch understood it too, but differently. Stretch’s size gave him the weather. M’s supply gave him gravity.

Together they formed a little system of orbiting needs. Men came near. Men asked for things. Men waited. Men owed. Men laughed too loudly. Men pretended they were just hanging around, as if camp were full of innocent pedestrians accidentally congregating near the same contraband-adjacent planets. I watched and kept my distance.

Distance is not fearful. Distance is mathematics.

If trouble has a radius, then survival begins by estimating circumference.

• How close can I stand before another man’s stupidity includes me?

• How many times can I laugh before laughter is interpreted as an allegiance?

• How much food can I accept before food becomes debt?

• How much curiosity can I show before curiosity becomes testimony?

These are not abstract questions in prison. They are practical geometry.

Never underestimate the danger of free food inside a locked institution.

Outside, free food means Costco samples. Inside, free food may come with invisible interest rates.

I had my own little economy. Notes. Books. Letters. Prayer. Exercise when possible. Observation. Those were my provisions. They did not taste like much, but they kept me from needing certain men too badly. That mattered because need is one of the first things prison weaponizes.

The system removes ordinary access to food, money, privacy, touch, information, affection, family, sex, internet, choice, and then watches men build substitute economies out of whatever remains. Ramen. Beans. Coffee. Spice. Pruno. Phone time. Protection. Rumors. Even hope.

Hope could be traded too.

A man could sell you a story about how to get out faster.

A man could sell you legal wisdom he had acquired from another man who had acquired it from another man who had once owned a jailhouse law book and had misunderstood most of it.

A man could sell you an introduction, a warning, a shortcut, a scheme, a job tip, a phone number, a fantasy. Prison was full of entrepreneurs. Some had business models. Some had addictions wearing business models as costumes.

The microwave kept humming. Men kept gathering.

I thought about American capitalism outside the fence and wondered if the difference was mostly packaging. Outside, men in suits sell poison with nicer typography. Inside, men in state clothing sell poison with more direct grammar. Outside, hunger becomes market segmentation. Inside, hunger stands near the microwave holding a plastic bowl.

That was why the whole thing fascinated me. Camp had reduced capitalism to its portable form. Supply. Demand. Scarcity. Addiction. Branding. Loyalty. Debt. Enforcement. Reputation.

Quality control if you were lucky. Customer retention if you were unlucky.

M did not need an MBA. He had a bunk, a network, a product line, and men willing to degrade themselves for relief.

The microwave did not care. It accepted all offerings equally.

Ramen from the lonely. Beans from the broke. Coffee from the sleepless. Cheese from the commissary aristocracy. Plastic bowls from men who had not yet understood that every object inside develops a social biography.

I stood there sometimes and watched the revolving food through the little window, thinking that if Moses came down from the mountain at Stewart Camp, he would not find a calf made of gold. He would find men arguing over whose noodles had thirty seconds left. And honestly, Moses might understand. He had led people through the wilderness. He knew what hunger does.

He knew how quickly deliverance becomes complaint when the stomach starts voting. I was no better. I wanted my soup hot too, hunger before belief.
